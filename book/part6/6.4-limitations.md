# Limitations

I should be plain about the limits of this book. My evidence is one system, authored by me, built
inside one vendor's agent ecosystem, deployed in production and used by me and my colleagues at
Purdue University. Every claim here stays scoped to that case.

One deep case is not a lonely one. Engineers working with agents have been converging,
independently and in numbers, on the moves this book teaches: heavy testing, specification-driven
development, an environment that enforces its own rules. Those reports corroborate that
practitioners independently reach for similar practices and report them useful. They do not show
that the practices *cause* the outcomes I claim; use and perceived value are not causal
effectiveness, and only a controlled study could close that gap. What this case supplies instead is
the mechanism's shape — how the moves compose, and why they hold against a fleet — seen in a system
I control end to end, with every layer in view. I built it as an instrument of inquiry as much as a
product: its quality had to matter for the evidence to matter, and I treated every failure as data
about the method rather than noise to clean up.

A single, self-built case carries limits that bear on transfer more than any question about writing
does. I name them plainly, because they are the terms on which someone else should read the
results.

- **A single operator with unusual authority.** One person held every role — author of the code,
  the governance, the models, and this book — and could change any of them at will. A team splits
  those roles across people who must negotiate; nothing here tested that friction.
- **Exceptional domain expertise and motivation.** I knew the domain deeply and cared about the
  outcome past the point most projects sustain. Both raise the ceiling on what governed autonomy
  reaches, and neither is typical.
- **Frontier proprietary models.** The fleet ran on the strongest closed models available. The
  results may not hold on weaker or open ones, and the vendor can change the model beneath me.
- **No human-team comparison.** I never ran the same work with a conventional team, so I cannot say
  the method beat that baseline — only that it worked in this setting.
- **No controlled counterfactual.** Nothing here isolates the method's contribution from the
  operator's, the domain's, or the model's. The case describes; it does not experiment.
- **Rapidly changing tools.** The substrate shifted under me across the build. A practice that paid
  off one month need not the next, and the book freezes a moving target.
- **Enormous operator time, at an unhealthy pace.** The build cost a volume of hours few could or
  should repeat. Part of what "worked" was sustained attention, not method.
- **Repository-specific metrics.** The code-size and churn figures are defined against this
  repository's conventions. They do not port cleanly to a project that counts differently.
- **Limited external adoption.** The product is used by me and a small group. It has not been
  stress-tested by a wide, indifferent user base.
- **An uncertain maintenance horizon.** I do not yet know whether the governed environment stays
  fit over years, or whether the compounding I claim survives a change of hands.
- **Researcher–builder confirmation bias.** I built the thing and I am assessing it. I tried to
  treat failures as data, but the risk that I read the evidence kindly is real and cannot be removed
  from a single-author case.
- **Partly retrospective models and measurements.** Some of the case's models and numbers were
  reconstructed after the fact rather than instrumented from the start, so they carry reconstruction
  error.

There is one further boundary, sharper and more personal: where I let the method draft this book,
and where I stopped it and wrote in my own voice. It belongs to me rather than to MAGE, so I have
set it aside as a field note below. It is an instance of the domain-governability moderator the
theory names: governed autonomy reaches farthest where a domain's important properties can be
represented and mechanically checked, and the final voice of a book is one place, for me, where that
check runs out.

> ### Field note — Why is this book's voice mine, not the fleet's? {#fieldnote-authorship-voice}
>
> I wrote this book the way I wrote the code. It has structured models of its own structure, and a
> drift gate holds those models to the prose — the same discipline I turned on the software. An LLM
> assisted with the first draft. The final version is entirely mine: every sentence rewritten in my
> own voice, and that rewrite is not a formality. It marks where I stopped the method.
>
> **My boundary for this book is authorship.** I am willing to delegate analytical implementation
> under governed checks, but not the final human voice through which I address the reader. Writing
> for other people is human-to-human communication, and voice is part of the message; it does not
> seem right to ask you to read my ideas at one remove, mediated by a machine that is not me. That is
> a choice about *this* book, made on my values — not a claim about what code or prose fundamentally
> are.
>
> It is tempting to draw the line as "code has no voice," but that is false. Code carries style,
> naming, explanatory structure, pedagogical intent, aesthetic character, and social consequences.
> And voice-bearing work can still use agent assistance under human authorship. The honest
> distinction is narrower and about *verifiability*: many critical properties of code — correctness,
> performance, security — admit executable or analytic validation to a degree authorial voice does
> not. Governed autonomy transfers most cleanly where a property can be checked, and writing for
> other people has a large share that cannot.
>
> This is one instance of a general scope condition. The domain-governability moderator says governed
> autonomy reaches as far as a domain's important properties can be represented and mechanically
> checked. When you set your own boundary, ask which properties of your artifact are checkable and
> which rest on judgment or voice. Delegate under checks where you can verify; hold the rest
> yourself. My authorship line is just one operator's reading of where that leaves the last voice of
> a book.
