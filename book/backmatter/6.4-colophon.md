# Colophon

> *A colophon is the note a book keeps about its own making — traditionally the last page, where the typeface, the press, and the hands that set the type were recorded before the reader closed the cover.*

This book was set in {{dt:font_display}} for its display and headings, {{dt:font_body}} for the body text — the quiet face that carries most of these pages — and {{dt:font_mono}} for code. The ground is a warm paper stock with a single {{dt:accent_name}} accent, and one eight-step type scale, addressed by role rather than by size, holds every heading, caption, and line of body to the same proportions. The design system has a name, the Umber Monograph, and it lives in one place: a design-tokens file [gh:book-models/design-tokens.json] the build reads at render time, so a change to a face or a color moves through the whole book at once instead of being re-typed on every page.

There was no separate typesetting pass. The book is a single typed intermediate representation — one structured model of chapters, sections, figures, and citations — projected two ways by the same build script [gh:book/build_book_html.py]: to HTML for the web edition, and to Typst for the PDF you may be holding, which `typst` then lays out. The web book and the print book are two views of one source; they cannot drift apart, because neither is authored by hand. References were kept in a BibTeX file [gh:book/references.bib] and pulled into the prose through inline citation markers, with a small pipeline resolving each marker and a gate that refuses to build if a citation has gone stale. The figures are hand-drawn SVGs — no plotting library sits behind them — each built against the same design tokens and each carrying a title and a description for readers who arrive at the page through a screen reader. One modest tool, written in nothing but the Python standard library so it runs on a fresh checkout [gh:catalog.py], validates the whole thing and builds it.

Those are the facts a colophon usually records. The more unusual one is that the book was maintained using the method it describes.

## The argument had somewhere outside the prose to live

The book's argument did not live only in its pages. It also lived in a set of small structured models kept alongside the manuscript. The central one is an argument-spine model [gh:book-models/argument_spine_declared.json]: the book's case written out as fourteen ordered claims, from *implementation has become abundant* through to *the method is grounded in one case*, with a record of which chapters advance each claim. Around it sit siblings — a chapter-shape model that grades how each chapter opens and closes, a model of the book's concepts and definitions, a claims register, a model of the flagship case studies, and a literature-positioning model [gh:book-models/lit_positioning_declared.json] in which every citation is an object — a key, the claims it backs, and the relation it bears to them — nested under the argument it supports rather than floating loose in a bibliography. A substantiation ledger [gh:book-models/substantiation.py] binds each factual number to the claim it stands behind, together with where it was observed and what its limits are, and a query walks the ledger to flag any claim about the world that has neither data nor literature behind it.

None of this is the book. The prose is the book. The models carried a compact account of how the parts depended on one another, and consistency between them was enforced the same way the rest of the method enforces things: a validate step, a handful of per-model checks, and the discipline that the models are declared by hand while their derived forms are generated — edit a generated file and the next build overwrites you. The purpose was never to replace writing. It was to give the argument somewhere outside the prose to live, so that a change in one place could be traced to its consequences in the others.

## Walking a revision from model to prose

Here is one time that mattered, drawn from the production record. Three of the architecture-view chapters — the logical view, the development view, and the physical view — were labeled in the argument-spine model as advancing both of the book's theses at once, the Modeling Thesis and the Alignment Thesis. A sensor that watches for a claim spread too thin across too many chapters flagged the Alignment Thesis: it was mapped onto more chapters than it was genuinely taught in, and those three views were the reason. They develop the Modeling Thesis. They do not really argue the Alignment one.

The fix was small, and the model made its reach exact. I dropped the Alignment label from the three chapters in the declared model. The model then named the three chapters implicated and nothing else, so the reconciliation came with a bounded worklist: re-read those three openings. They turned out never to mention Alignment at all — the labels had been aspirational, the prose was already honest — so not a sentence needed changing. The drift check confirmed the count had fallen back within bounds, and the derived spine regenerated from the corrected source. Revision was not automated. What the machinery did was make the reach of a change visible and finite, which is a smaller and more useful thing than deciding what the change should be.

## Language models, openly and on a short leash

Large language models were part of making this book, and it would be coy not to say so plainly. I used Anthropic's Claude — both Opus and the lighter Fable — and OpenAI's ChatGPT 5.6-Thinking. They helped find adjacent research traditions and surface where a literature already existed; they proposed candidate citations; they helped compare the argument with neighboring work, and analyze whether a model and its prose still agreed; they drafted figure concepts, captions, and the accessible descriptions that ride under each SVG; and they turned rough material into candidate drafts I could argue with.

They were assistance, not authority, and the difference showed up most sharply in the bibliography. A language model will produce plausible bibliographic detail, including plausible detail that is wrong. One candidate citation came back with an author list that looked complete and was short by two names; checking it against the source restored them and brought the paper to its actual eleven. Another time a benchmark was on its way to being credited to the wrong lab — a "Pro" variant that belongs to one group, easily confused with a similarly named withdrawal from another. So every citation, quotation, number, and factual claim was verified against the primary source before it was allowed to stay. The model could tell me where to look. It could not stand behind what I found there.

## Dictation, and the rewrite that mattered

Much of the prose began as speech. The working loop was simple and human: I would dictate an idea in my own cadence, name the files it belonged in, let the speech be transcribed, and then repair the transcript — using this repository's own writing discipline, the self-communicate lexicon [gh:plugin/mage/skills/self-communicate/writing/audit.md], the same voice and rhetoric guides the book's prose is held to — before discussing where the material should sit in the outline and starting again.

That repair step was not cosmetic. Transcribed and generated prose drifts toward a recognizable set of faults: repetition, false symmetry, the reflexive rule of three, generic transitions, a confidence the evidence has not earned, a conclusion restated once more after it has already landed. The self-communicate audit is the tool that names those tells and removes them — the book's prose was cleaned by the repository's own writing skill, which is about as close to eating one's own cooking as a colophon is likely to confess. Not every sentence was made the same way. Some survived as structure and lost their words; some kept their words; a few began as nothing but a blank page turning into something I could disagree with. Dictation supplied the thought in my cadence, and the rewriting made it mine.

## What the machinery could not do

The machinery had a firm boundary, and it is worth stating without hedging. It could trace a claim through the book; it could not decide whether the claim was true. It could hold two representations in agreement; it could not turn that agreement into correctness. **A consistency check can show that the model and prose agree. It cannot show that either is right.**

Everything that mattered most stayed on the human side of that line: choosing the argument, deciding what counted as evidence and where the evidence stopped, reading a criticism for what it was actually saying, accepting one revision and refusing another, judging when a claim had grown too strong, and standing behind the finished book. The models made those judgments easier to carry through hundreds of pages. They did not make them, and they could not have.

Traditional colophons record the typeface that carried the words. This one records that too — {{dt:font_display}} across the headings, {{dt:font_body}} through the body, the old craft by which a manuscript becomes a book. It also records the stranger fact underneath: the argument had somewhere outside the prose to live, and that map is part of what kept the words, over a long revision, from drifting apart.
