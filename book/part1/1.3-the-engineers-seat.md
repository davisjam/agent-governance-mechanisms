# The Engineer's Seat

<!-- point: mage-keeps-lifecycle-reassigns-one-seat | MAGE keeps the lifecycle; it reassigns one seat. | terms: selc, lifecycle-phases -->
By now the method may sound like a new discipline, with new phases and new
artifacts. It is not. It is the oldest structure in the field with one seat
reassigned. This chapter draws that structure for the reader who has never
worked it, and states the claim the rest of the book stands on: **MAGE does
not remove the engineering lifecycle. It changes the role of the engineer
within it.**

## The lifecycle, briefly

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | The lifecycle, briefly — the six-activity list and the required waterfall footnote sit under one heading; the running prose is lean -->

<!-- section-terms: lifecycle-phases -->

<!-- point: engineering-shares-six-general-activities | Every engineering discipline shares six activities. | terms: lifecycle-phases -->
<!-- index-def: lifecycle-phases -->
Engineering ran on the same handful of activities long before software
existed. Build a bridge, a chip, or an airframe and you do the same six
things, in some order. They are the general engineering cycle, and software
inherits them whole.

- **Requirements** — find out what the system must do and for whom. The
  people who need it usually cannot state it; drawing it out of them is the work.
- **Specification** — say what the system will do, precisely enough that
  you can later check whether it does.
- **Design** — decide the structure: the parts, the seams between them,
  who is responsible for what, and how the whole meets its constraints.
- **Implementation** — build the thing. For software, write the code.
- **Validation** — show that what got built meets the specification:
  tests, analysis, review.
- **Maintenance** — everything after first release, which is most of the
  life: change the system without breaking what it already does.

<!-- point: software-loosens-physics-copies-free-stays-malleable | Software loosens physics, copies free, stays malleable. | terms: lifecycle-phases -->
What makes *software's* lifecycle its own is easy to forget, so name it. A
program answers to far fewer physical constraints than a bridge — no steel,
no fatigue, no tolerance the material forces on you. Once written it copies
for nothing, so one good build serves millions at no marginal cost. And it
stays changeable long after release: the maintenance window, where most of
the life is spent, stays open in a way a physical artifact's never does.
Looser physics, free copies, changeability under maintenance — these are why
the same six activities play out so differently here.

<!-- point: sketch-defers-to-standard-texts | The sketch defers to the standard texts. | terms: lifecycle-phases -->
That is a sketch, and a sketch is all this book needs. Each activity has a
literature of its own, and the standard texts — Sommerville's *Software
Engineering*, Pressman and Maxim's *Software Engineering: A Practitioner's
Approach* — treat them in the depth they deserve. This book will not. It
assumes only the sketch: six activities, each answering a different
question, together carrying a system from a need to a running, changing
artifact.

<!-- point: process-models-reorder-never-replace | Process models reorder the activities, never replace them. | terms: lifecycle-phases -->
The process models differ only in how they order these activities. Waterfall
runs them once, front to back. Iterative processes run them in loops, days
or hours wide. The activities are the same either way; the argument was only
ever about the ordering, and this book takes no position on it. What it takes
a position on is who sits where.

> *A footnote on "waterfall."* The pure, once-through waterfall is usually
> credited to Winston Royce's 1970 paper, "Managing the Development of Large
> Software Systems." The credit is backwards. Royce drew the strict
> sequential model in order to argue *against* it — he called it flawed and
> said no sane organization would build that way, and he called for cycles,
> for iteration between the steps. His cycles were simply years long, sized
> to the Department-of-Defense projects of the day, whose scope still runs to
> years now. The strawman outlived the argument that built it.

## The seat that moved

<!-- section-terms: engineers-seat, selc -->

<!-- point: conventionally-one-role-holds-every-seat | Conventionally one role holds every seat. | terms: engineers-seat -->
In the conventional lifecycle one role holds every seat: requirements,
specification, design, implementation, validation. Of the six, one dominates
the calendar — implementation. Writing the code was the expensive part, so
the discipline arranged itself around the writing, and the seat that ate the
calendar defined the job.

<!-- point: engineer-authors-intent-developer-writes-code | The engineer authors intent; the developer writes code. | terms: engineers-seat -->
<!-- index-def: engineers-seat -->
Two words for two jobs, and this book keeps them apart. The **engineer** is
the person who authors the intent, chooses the structure, and judges the
result — requirements, specification, design, validation. The **developer**
is the person who implements it: writes the code that realizes the design.
How a shop divides that labor is its own affair — a lone founder may hold
both; a large organization splits them across people, teams, and titles like
tech lead and architect, and contracts parts of the build out entirely. The
book prescribes none of it. It needs the two words only to name the seats.

<!-- point: engineer-technician-split-kept-collapsing | The engineer/technician split kept collapsing back together. | terms: engineers-seat -->
Older engineering fields draw a clean line between the engineer who designs
and the technician who builds. Software spent decades trying to draw the same
line — an engineer class over a developer class — and across much of the
industry the complexity of system-building defeated the attempt. Knowing a
system well enough to design it kept requiring you to build it, and building
it kept requiring the judgment that was supposed to sit a rank away. The
seats kept collapsing back together.

<!-- point: mage-reassigns-only-the-developer-seat | MAGE reassigns exactly one seat: the developer's. | terms: engineers-seat, selc -->
MAGE changes exactly one thing in that picture. The developer's seat goes to
the agents. The fleet writes the code, and the human keeps the seats that
were always the engineering: stating what to build — requirements,
specification, design, authored as models the fleet can reason over —
judging what comes back, and holding the whole to a standard the fleet
cannot lower.

<!-- point: role-shrinks-nothing-judgment-more-consequential | The role shrinks nothing; judgment grows more consequential. | terms: engineers-seat -->
Do not read this as the engineer doing less. The preface said code got cheap
and judgment did not; here is where that lands. Every hour the fleet saves on
implementation is throughput pointed wherever your design points it, and a
wrong design now gets built out at full speed. The judgment seats were always
the consequential ones. Now they are the job.

## SDLC to SELC

<!-- noqa: book-section-cap | SDLC to SELC — the word count is inflated by the inlined figure's SVG a11y description text and its caption, not wall-of-text prose -->

<!-- section-terms: selc -->

<!-- point: sdlc-names-the-delegated-activity | SDLC names the activity the human no longer performs. | terms: selc -->
The field's own name for the lifecycle gives the old arrangement away: SDLC,
the software *development* life cycle. The name centers development because
development was the bottleneck; you name the cycle after the seat where the
calendar goes.

<!-- point: rename-to-selc-engineering-life-cycle | Rename it SELC: the software engineering life cycle. | terms: selc -->
<!-- index-def: selc -->
So I propose a rename: **SELC**, the software *engineering* life cycle. The
case is short. The D is delegated — development belongs to the fleet now, and
a name should not center a seat the human has left. The E is what remains,
and it was always the point: stating intent precisely, structuring the
system, deciding what proof a change owes, and holding the line. SELC names
the job that is left, which is the job this book teaches. [ref:sdlc-to-selc]
draws the two arrangements.

<!-- point: delegation-keeps-accountability-intact | Delegating the keystrokes keeps accountability intact. | terms: selc, whose-fault -->
Delegating the keystrokes delegates nothing else. The engineer who no longer
types the code is still answerable for it, the way an engineer was always
answerable for what a chosen tool produced. The back matter returns to what
that responsibility becomes ([Implications for Software
Engineering](6.0-implications-for-se.html)); for now it is enough that the
seat moved and the accountability did not.

## A different seat is a different discipline

<!-- section-terms: selc, engineers-seat -->

<!-- point: reassigned-seat-remakes-the-discipline | Same phases; the reassigned seat remakes the discipline. | terms: selc -->
Same lifecycle, then; no new phases. But the reassignment still remakes the
discipline, because the intent now has to be legible to a probabilistic
machine. A specification a human developer could interpret charitably must
now be precise enough for that machine to execute — which is why this book
authors spec and design as typed models ([The Model
Zoo](3.1-the-executable-zoo.html)). Validation at fleet velocity outruns any
human reader, so it moves into the environment as gates the fleet cannot
skip. And maintenance under a workforce with no memory is, I will argue, best
run as governance: rules that hold because the environment enforces them, not
because anyone remembers them. Whether large organizations keep their teams,
their titles, and their handoffs through this shift, the book does not say —
nobody knows yet.

<!-- point: rest-of-book-equips-the-engineers-seat | The rest of the book equips the engineer's seat. | terms: selc -->
That is the map of the rest of the book: it equips the engineer's seat, phase
by phase. And it starts where the loss of the developer's seat bites first.
The engineer's grip on implementation is now indirect — you govern the
machine that writes the code, not the code itself. So where do you reach in
to do it? That is the question the next part opens on.
